Since April I have been building a production SaaS almost entirely with coding
agents. One person, real users, five months of history. What I did not have was
a single number about whether any of it worked.

So I had an agent write a script to backfill delivery metrics out of git. It
took a morning. By the afternoon three of its four findings had been retracted,
using its own output.

I am going to be exact about who did what in this piece, because the subject is
misplaced confidence and it would be absurd to be loose about it. **The agent
wrote the script, chose the definitions, and produced the findings. The belief
that made the wrong finding attractive was mine.** The retraction came out of
the same agent, on the same day, before I had reviewed anything.

That sequence is the interesting part, so it is what this is about.

## The number that survived

**Of 1,802 non-merge commits between 16 April and 19 August, 1,648 were
agent-touched — 91.5%.** A commit counts if Claude authored it or co-authored
it, and both are recorded by the tooling rather than reconstructed later.

It holds up because it is almost too simple to get wrong. It also means less
than it sounds like: it counts **participation, not authorship.** A commit an
agent started and I rewrote still carries the trailer. The sentence it supports
is _an agent had a hand in nine of ten commits_, and never _an agent wrote nine
tenths of the code._

That distinction is not pedantry. The second sentence is the one people want to
quote, and it is not in the data.

## The metric that did not survive

The script also produced a rework rate — some signal about whether all that
volume was being paid for later. It was defined as a commit meeting **two**
conditions:

1. Its subject line says it is a fix or a revert.
2. It touches a file whose previous commit landed less than seven days earlier.

The second condition is the one that makes this look like careful work. Subject
lines alone are noisy — routine maintenance on year-old code reads as a fix.
Recency appears to isolate the case that actually matters: churn on work that is
still warm. The script's own header said so, in as many words: **both halves
are needed.**

Nobody had checked whether the second half did anything.

| Window  | Commits passing the gate |
| ------- | -----------------------: |
| 1 day   |                    75.6% |
| 7 days  |                **89.6%** |
| 30 days |                    93.7% |

The gate removed about one commit in ten. At a **one-day** window it still
passed three quarters of them. The working set in a single-developer repository
is small and hot, so nearly everything touches something recent. The careful
second condition was doing almost nothing, and the compound metric was the
subject-line rate wearing a second condition for credibility.

## The part that actually matters

A weak filter is embarrassing. A filter that moves with the thing you are
measuring is worse, and that is what this one does.

| Month | Commits | Gate pass rate |
| ----- | ------: | -------------: |
| April |     125 |          72.8% |
| May   |     152 |          73.7% |
| June  |     482 |          89.0% |
| July  |     975 |      **94.4%** |

More commits mechanically means a higher chance that any given file was touched
recently. The gate does not hold still while volume changes — **it loosens.**
So a metric gated on it drifts upward with volume on its own, with no change in
quality whatsoever.

Which brings me to the finding I nearly published.

## It confirmed something I had already written down

The headline was: _rework rate co-moves with commit volume, peaking in July at
the height of agent parallelism._

July was the month I had the most agent sessions running at once, and the month
with the most unreviewed work piling up. And weeks earlier, in my own operating
notes, I had written that parallelism is a multiplier on whatever it is pointed
at, including a mistake. Here was a chart agreeing with me, out of my own
repository, produced by a process I had not yet inspected.

I would have believed it. That is the whole problem.

Two things were wrong with it.

**It is not even monotonic.** May runs a 34.2% fix rate against June's 23.4%,
on a third of the commits. One counterexample in five data points is not a
relationship.

**And the confound points the same way as the hypothesis.** If the gate loosens
as volume rises, then "rework rises with volume" is what the instrument
produces whether or not it is true of the code. The finding and the artifact
predict the same chart, so the chart cannot tell them apart — and neither could
I, from looking at it.

That is the shape worth watching for. A metric that fails randomly gets caught.
A metric that fails _in the direction you already expect_ gets published.

## The comparison that also had to go

The other tempting number was agent commits against human commits — same
history, same repository, one rate each. They came out nearly identical, which
would have made a very quotable sentence.

The human population is 154 commits out of 1,802. It includes dependabot, and
my own commits that simply lost the trailer. And it is doing visibly different
work: 19.6% documentation against 11.5%, 7.3% schema against 1.9%.

Two groups doing different things at a ten-to-one size ratio cannot be compared
by setting their rates side by side. There is no control group here. There was
never going to be one — I am one person, and I cannot run the counterfactual
where I wrote the same five months by hand.

## What we kept instead of deleting it

Every column is still generated: the subject-line rate, the gate pass rate, and
the fix rate inside and outside the gate. That last pair is the direct evidence
the gate does not discriminate — the two run together in May and July and
**invert** in August.

Keeping them makes the confound visible to the next reader instead of inherited
by them. What did get deleted is the column _name_. It used to be
`rework_rate_pct`, and a column called that is a claim. It is
`fix_subject_pct` now, which is all it ever measured.

Three things are still absent and staying absent: pull-request cycle time,
time-to-green, and review-to-merge latency. Git holds no timestamp for when a
pull request opened, when its checks first went green, or when a review landed.
Those need the forge's API. I would rather have a gap than a proxy assembled
out of commit dates, because once it is drawn on a chart it looks exactly like
a measurement.

## The check that would have caught it in five minutes

If you are building a metric for your own AI adoption — and the pressure to
produce one is considerable right now — here is the cheap test that should run
before anyone reads the output.

**Before reporting any conditional rate, report the base rate of each condition
on its own.** What fraction of _everything_ passes filter A? What fraction
passes filter B? If one of them passes 90% of your population, it is not a
filter, it is decoration, and your compound metric is the other condition with
better marketing.

**Then ask whether either condition moves with your independent variable.** If
you are measuring something against volume, and one of your filters gets more
permissive as volume rises, the relationship you are about to report may be
arithmetic rather than evidence.

Neither check is sophisticated. Both take minutes.

## The uncomfortable bit, which is the reason to write any of this down

My working rule is that agents do the implementation and I supply the judgment.
The measurement layer is where that rule is supposed to matter most, because a
number is the thing that survives into a slide with none of its caveats
attached.

What happened is that the implementation, the definition, the finding, **and
the check that killed the finding** all came from the agent. My contribution
was a prior that would have made the wrong answer easy to accept, and a review
step that had not happened yet.

I do not think the lesson is that agents cannot be trusted with measurement.
The instrument caught its own error inside four hours, which is faster than
most review cycles I have worked in. But it is not the division of labour I
describe when I describe how I work, and noticing that is worth more than the
metric was.

The argument for measuring an agent-operated system at all is that the claims
about it are otherwise unfalsifiable. That argument only holds if the
measurements are falsifiable too — which means occasionally one of them has to
die in public.
