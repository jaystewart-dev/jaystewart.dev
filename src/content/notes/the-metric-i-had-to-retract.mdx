Since March I have not written a line of code by hand. I run a small software
product — real users, one person — where AI does the building and my job is
deciding what gets built and whether it is any good.

Five months of that, and I had no idea whether it was working. So I asked the AI
to go through the project's history and measure it.

It gave me an answer. The answer was wrong, and it was wrong in the most
dangerous way available: it agreed with me.

## What I wanted to know

A simple thing. When you work fast, do you end up fixing more of your own mess
afterwards?

Every change to the project is saved with a short note describing it. Some of
those notes say things like "fix" or "undo". So you can count them, and get a
rough sense of how much of the work is repair rather than progress.

Counting the word "fix" on its own is too crude, though. Tidying up something
you wrote a year ago is not the same as patching something you broke last
Tuesday. So the measurement used two rules. To count as repair, a change had to
**say it was a fix**, and it had to **touch something that had been worked on in
the last week**.

Two rules sounds more careful than one. That was the whole problem.

## The answer I liked

The result came back: the busier the month, the more repair work.

The busiest month by far had been July, when I had the most AI sessions running
at once. And weeks earlier I had written a note to myself saying that running
lots of things in parallel multiplies whatever you are doing — including your
mistakes.

So here was a chart, made from my own project, agreeing with something I had
already decided was true.

I believed it immediately. That is the part worth paying attention to.

## What checking it found

The second rule — _touched something worked on in the last week_ — was supposed
to be the careful one. It turns out it excluded almost nothing. **Nearly nine in
ten changes passed it.** Even when I narrowed it from a week to a single day, it
still let three quarters through.

The reason is obvious in hindsight. I am one person working on one project, so
I am always circling the same handful of files. Practically everything I touch
was touched recently. The rule that was supposed to be a filter was a formality.

Then the worse part. Because the rule is about _recent_ activity, it gets easier
to pass the busier you are. In a quiet month it let through seven changes in ten.
In the busiest month, more than nine.

So the measurement gets bigger when I get busier — **on its own, whether or not
anything has actually gone wrong.**

Which means my finding, "working faster produces more repair work," is what this
method produces no matter what. It was not a discovery about my work. It was a
description of how busy I had been, wearing a costume.

An everyday version: suppose you measured how many of your emails were urgent by
counting the ones that say "urgent" _and_ arrived in the last hour. On a busy
day, everything arrived in the last hour — so busy days automatically look more
urgent, and you would conclude that being busy makes people needier. It doesn't.
Your ruler is just made of the same stuff you are trying to measure.

## The kind of wrong that gets published

A measurement that produces nonsense gets caught, because someone looks at it
and says that cannot be right.

A measurement that produces the answer you were already expecting does not get
that look. It gets nodded at, and repeated, and eventually it ends up on a slide
with the caveats stripped off.

That is the whole lesson, and it is not really about software:

**A result that surprises you gets checked. A result that flatters you gets
used.**

The uncomfortable footnote is that the AI which built the flawed measurement is
also the one that found the flaw. Not on its own initiative — it found it
because checking the method was a scheduled job, with time set aside, and the
instruction was _try to break this_ rather than _does this look right?_ My own
review had not happened yet. It was still on the list when the whole thing fell
over.

I do not think that means AI cannot be trusted with measurement. I think it
means the checking step has to be somebody's actual job, has to happen before
anyone sees the number, and has to be phrased as an attack. "Review this" tends
to come back agreeing with you — and agreement was already the problem.

## What I do now, before believing any number

Two questions, and neither requires knowing anything about software.

**First: how much does each rule actually rule out?** If one of your conditions
lets nine out of ten things through, it is not doing any work. Your careful
two-part measurement is a one-part measurement with a decoration attached.

**Second: does anything in the method change along with the thing being
studied?** If you are asking whether being busy causes problems, and part of
your method quietly gets more generous when you are busy, then the answer is
built into the question.

Both take five minutes. Neither is clever. They get skipped because a method
that _sounds_ careful is exactly the kind nobody tests — and because the result
agreed with what everybody already thought.

The reason to measure any of this is that otherwise the claims people make about
working with AI cannot be checked at all. That only means something if the
measurements can be checked too. Which occasionally requires one of them to die
in public.
